## Abstract

**In-modulator encryption XORs each output bit of a delta-sigma modulator with a keystream, so that a sensor encrypts its readings without a decimator. The secure delta-sigma modulator (S-DSM) of Yasar *et al.* [1] does so with a 16-bit key held on the chip, a linear keystream without a nonce and unauthenticated frames. This work keeps its front end and its bit-level XOR and adds four ideas new to in-modulator encryption: a 128-bit key that is never stored, regenerated at every power-up from static random-access memory (SRAM) cells used as a physically unclonable function (PUF), through a fuzzy extractor sized from the cell's simulated error rates and secret budget; the keystream and a 32-bit message authentication code of every frame from one Grain-128a core; a chip nonce, hashed from 64 power-up reads of the same cells, in every initialisation vector; and a one-time enrolment at −40 and 85 °C. Both designs are evaluated with open-source tools only. A bit-level model of the link reproduces the two-channel measurement of [1] (48.2 % and 50.0 % of bits differ, against 49.8 % and 50.05 % measured) and takes its circuit parameters from post-layout SKY130 simulations of the modulator's StrongARM comparator and of a six-transistor SRAM cell at 45 process, voltage and temperature (PVT) conditions. Three findings are new. First, analog differences between chips cannot replace a key: although the comparator holds the loop at 60.3–60.4 dB signal-to-noise-and-distortion ratio at every condition, a replica attack breaks all 360 simulated chip pairs, and comparator noise stops the attack only at 197× its largest simulated value. Second, key reliability and nonce freshness conflict: the cell noise that the fuzzy extractor corrects is too small to make one read a fresh nonce, so the nonce is hashed from 64 reads. Third, at the fast-nMOS corners some SRAM cells reverse their preferred state with temperature (27.8 % bit errors at 85 °C); only enrolment at both temperatures keeps the key failure rate below 10⁻⁶ at every corner, while retaining 284–352 secret bits. With an ageing law scaled from published NBTI measurements (the PDK has none), an array kept powered with its power-up values loses that target within 26 days, whereas writing back the inverse of each read makes the key more reliable with age, at the cost of the randomness the nonce draws from the same cells. In this model the S-DSM fails 11 of 15 attacks (three under stated assumptions); the proposal stops 14 at the working setting of the system simulations and all 15, 10 resisted and 5 detected, with the two-temperature enrolment. Power analysis lies outside the threat model, and a leakage test detects key-dependent leakage in both designs as built: in [1] at every clock of every frame, in the proposal only while the key is loaded and mixed with the initialisation vector at the start of a frame. In one SKY130 flow the proposal's keystream block takes 9,869 gate equivalents (GE) and 64.3 µW at 10 MHz, 3.6× the S-DSM, and has the best (lowest) figure of merit, energy per bit per security bit: 2.21, against 5.09 for Trivium and 14.1 for the S-DSM. At an oversampling ratio of 50, the proposed sensor sends 1.44–1.60× fewer bits per reading than the decimate-then-Trivium design of [1]. A key-generator compiler turns the reliability and secrecy specification into a 19,128 GE decoder, bit-identical to the model in 72 of 72 RTL reconstructions. A mixed-level simulation of the complete sensor regenerates the key from transistor-level cells bit for bit, and 3 of 2,000 transistor-level comparator decisions differ from the model, consistent with the comparator noise (2.4 expected). Every result is computed live in one Colab session, except a few long simulations, which are shown from recorded runs that a switch or a script reproduces.**

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Proposal: what prior work leaves open and what this work adds**
>
> The S-DSM of [1], and the prior work that Section III reviews, **leave a secure low-power sensor link open on five counts**. This work proposes an answer to each: the first four are ideas new to in-modulator encryption, and the fifth is an evaluation that no prior work of this kind provides.
>
> 1. **The key.** *Gap:* the S-DSM holds a 16-bit key on the chip, which exhaustive search finds and which an attacker who obtains a chip can read if it is stored in non-volatile memory, as this work assumes (Section II-E); silicon PUF key generators avoid a stored key but have not keyed a sensor link. *Proposal:* **a 128-bit key that is never stored, regenerated at every power-up from the chip's own SRAM array** through a BCH fuzzy extractor and a hash, and held only in volatile registers (Section IV).
> 2. **The keystream and the frame.** *Gap:* the S-DSM's 16-bit LFSR keystream is linear, so that Berlekamp-Massey recovers it from 32 keystream bits, and its frames are not authenticated, so that forged, altered or replayed frames are accepted. *Proposal:* **the keystream and a 32-bit MAC of every frame from one Grain-128a core** at the modulator's bit rate, with the front end, the modulator and the bit-level XOR of [1] unchanged.
> 3. **Freshness.** *Gap:* the S-DSM has no nonce, so that its keystream repeats after every power-up. *Proposal:* **a chip nonce, hashed from 64 power-up reads of the same SRAM cells**, in every initialisation vector beside the receiver's session nonce and a frame counter, so that a replayed session nonce repeats a keystream only on a 31-bit hash collision.
> 4. **Temperature.** *Gap* (**found in this work**): the key must hold over the sensor's temperature range, but at the fast-nMOS corners up to 26 % of the SRAM cells reverse their preferred state at 85 °C, so that an enrolment at 25 °C cannot meet the key-failure target at every corner (Sections VII-F and VIII-F). *Proposal:* **a one-time enrolment at −40 and 85 °C that keeps only the cells stable and equal at both temperatures**, sized from the simulated cells (a BCH code that corrects up to t = 17 flipped cells, t being its correction limit).
> 5. **The evaluation.** *Gap:* [1] evaluates an eavesdropper with a replica chip and two cryptanalyses on measured silicon at one condition, and no prior evaluation of such a link is open and executable. *Proposal:* **both designs evaluated from transistor to system in one reproducible notebook**, with open-source tools only: a model consistent with the measurements of [1] (48.2 % and 50.0 % of bits differ between two channels, against 49.8 % and 50.05 % measured; Section VI); circuit parameters from extracted SKY130 layouts of a StrongARM comparator at 45 PVT conditions and a 6T SRAM cell at 15 (Section VII); their effect on accuracy, power and security (Section VIII); 15 attacks, with **a leakage test** of where each design exposes its key to power analysis (Sections IX-D and X-H); RTL verified against published test vectors, synthesised, placed and routed in SKY130 in the same low-power form for every compared design and ranked by **a proposed figure of merit** that scales no number (Section X); **a key-generator compiler** from a reliability and secrecy specification to a decoder verified bit for bit against the model and placed and routed (Section XI); and **one simulation of the complete sensor across abstraction levels**, from transistor-level SRAM cells and comparators through the RTL decoder and cipher to the receiver's MAC check (Section XII).
>
> **Summary of results.** The original design fails 11 of the 15 attacks; the proposed design resists 10 and detects 5 with the two-temperature enrolment (9 and 5 at the working setting t = 45, where the helper data leave too few secret bits, A11). **The analog circuits do not secure the original design**: comparator noise protects it only from 197× its worst PVT value, at 7.5 dB of signal-to-noise-and-distortion ratio (SNDR). The extracted SRAM cell has about one third of the assumed noise-to-mismatch ratio (0.0225 against 0.06), so that a 600-cell code enrolled at 25 °C needs t = 39 for the $10^{-6}$ target on the probability that the key fails to regenerate where the secrecy budget allows at most t = 35, and no searched code lets a 25 °C enrolment meet the target at all five corners; the two-temperature enrolment does, with t = 17.

## Key contributions and principal results

The contributions as they stand in the published run, in the order in which the notebook establishes them:

1. **PUF-keyed, authenticated in-modulator encryption** (Section IV, Fig. 1). No key is held in on-chip non-volatile memory: a 128-bit key is regenerated at every power-up from SRAM-PUF cells through a BCH fuzzy extractor and a hash, and drives Grain-128a [6] with its MAC, a frame counter, a session nonce and a chip nonce. The analog front end, the modulator and the bit-level XOR of [1] are unchanged.
2. **A bit-level model (digital blocks bit-exact), checked against the published measurements of [1] before use** (Sections V and VI). Grain-128a reproduces all 22 published test-vector values; the two-channel test of [1] gives 50.0 % and 48.2 % of bits that differ (50.05 % and 49.8 % measured), and the bit counts per reading reproduce the energy ratios of its Fig. 4(b) within 0.18 %, a check of the bit accounting rather than of the model (no radio number used). With its simplified dither, the model reaches the independence value of its test sine (48.2 % against 48.4 %; 49.8 % measured in [1]) with a small offset between the channels; [1] attributes the disagreement to its dither, which the model does not contradict.
3. **Post-layout SKY130 comparator and SRAM cell** (Section VII). A StrongARM comparator and a 6T SRAM-PUF cell are drawn, DRC- and LVS-clean, extracted and simulated in ngspice (tt, 27 °C, 1.8 V): $\sigma_\mathrm{n}$ = 0.575 mV, $\sigma_\mathrm{os}$ = 4.37 mV and 199 fJ per decision; noise-to-mismatch ratio $s_{25}$ = 0.0225 and bit-error rates of 1.37, 0.99 and 3.14 % at −40, 25 and 85 °C (recorded Monte Carlo, 1,024 cells).
4. **45 PVT conditions** (Sections VII-F and VIII). The comparator spans $\sigma_\mathrm{n}$ 0.37–0.81 mV, $\sigma_\mathrm{os}$ 4.01–5.85 mV and 144–822 fJ, and, with the comparator noise of each of the 45 PVT conditions (behavioural model; no offset, one noise seed each), the simulated sensor gives 60.3–60.4 dB SNDR. At the fast-nMOS corners the SRAM cell's mismatch rotates with temperature (up to 50° at fs), and 25.6 % of the cells at fs reverse their preferred state at 85 °C; a recorded mechanism check attributes these flips to the latch, not to the testbench.
5. **Full-scan attacker statistics with Clopper–Pearson intervals** (Section VIII). An attacker who scans every 64-bit window of four frames breaks 360 of 360 simulated chip pairs of the original design at the 45 PVT conditions. Comparator noise stops it (0 of 100 pairs; at most 3.6 % per pair at 95 % confidence) only from $\sigma_\mathrm{n}$ = 160 mV, 197× its largest PVT value, at a cost of 7.5 dB of SNDR; comparator offset, integrator noise and capacitor mismatch never do within their sweeps.
6. **Key sizing with a bounded secret budget** (Sections VII-E and VIII-F). With 600 cells enrolled at 25 °C, no BCH code meets both a key-failure probability below $10^{-6}$ over 0–85 °C and the 192 secret bits that a 128-bit key with a hash margin requires: t = 45 is the working setting of the system simulations and leaves 94–137 secret bits. A one-time enrolment at −40 and 85 °C meets $10^{-6}$ for at least 99.7 % of the modelled chips at every corner with t = 17, leaving 284–352 secret bits at fs, the corner with the lowest min-entropy bound (935 cells for 600 kept cells).
7. **A 15-attack evaluation with an atlas** (Section IX). In the model, the original S-DSM fails 11 of the 15 attacks. The proposed design stops 14 of the 15 at the working setting t = 45; the fifteenth, helper-data leakage (A11), leaves 94 secret bits at the 95 % lower bound of the min-entropy against the 192 required. The one-time enrolment at −40 and 85 °C raises this to 284–352, and the proposed design then resists 10 attacks and detects and rejects the other 5 (four in simulation; the clone, A9, by design, through the unclonability of the PUF). Of the 15 verdicts, 7 are simulated, 4 analytic and 4 hold by design. One atlas figure draws all 30 verdicts.
8. **A low-power keystream block, compared under the same conditions** (Section X). The three bit-level designs built in this flow (S-DSM, Trivium and the proposed design) are each given the same low-power techniques (radix-16 parallel state update, clock gating) and simulated at gate level under the same conditions; the decimate-then-encrypt baseline keeps its one-bit-per-clock cipher, with its decimator power estimated per GE, and [1]'s blocks are its measured values. The proposed design's block: 9,869 GE on `sky130_fd_sc_hd`, placed live and routed in a recorded run (`ROUTE_BLOCK`) on 399 × 399 µm with 0 DRC violations, LVS match, OpenRCX extraction and static timing at three corners (setup slack +85.5 ns at ss, hold slack +0.12 ns at ff); 16 antenna violations remain. In gate-level simulation it dissipates 64.3 µW at 10 MHz (73.4 µW routed), 6.5× less than as written for one bit per clock and 3.6× the S-DSM in the same form. A proposed figure of merit, FOM = $E_\mathrm{bit}/(E_\mathrm{NAND2}\,S_\Sigma)$, the energy per bit of the security logic over the library's NAND2 switching energy and the security bits delivered over the goals met, ranks the blocks re-synthesised in the same flow without scaling any number or using any modelled one: 2.21 for the proposed design against 5.09 for Trivium and 14.1 for the S-DSM.
9. **A key-generator compiler** (Section XI). From a reliability and secrecy specification to a generated decoder, verified in RTL simulation, synthesised and routed: a 25 °C enrolment cannot meet $10^{-6}$ at all five corners with any code searched; a two-temperature enrolment can. The key generator on which the security claims rest (enrolment at −40 and 85 °C, BCH t = 17 on 600 of 935 cells, 284 secret bits at the 95 % lower bound of the min-entropy) is compiled as sized: its generated decoder of 19,128 GE matches the model bit for bit in 72 of 72 reconstructions, and the designed parts of the complete security subsystem total 47,292 GE. A smaller design for 25 and 85 °C (r = 3, t = 4, 1,395 cells, 8,984 GE decoder) meets the same target under the cell models, but its enrolment does not include the cold.
10. **A full-chain mixed-level simulation of the complete sensor, with open-source tools only** (Section XII). Extracted SRAM cells and post-layout StrongARM comparators inside the modulator loop run at transistor level together with the RTL key decoder and Grain-128a/MAC block; the key is regenerated from transistor-level cells, the comparator's decisions agree with the comparator model within its noise, and a recorded 30,000-clock replica attack recovers the original design's key from 8 of 80 windows and the proposed design's from none.

The transmission result is stated with its limits, as bits, since no radio is designed in this work (with the radio of [1] as an example, one frame per minute averages 28.0 µW for the proposed design against 9.2 µW for on-sensor decimation): at the oversampling ratio of 50 used for every accuracy result, the proposed design sends **1.44–1.60× fewer bits** than the decimate-and-Trivium design of [1] (the 2.66× obtained at OSR 30, the setting of [1]'s headline 2.67×, does not hold at OSR 50), whereas an **information-equivalent conventional design**, which decimates on the sensor and encrypts 16-bit words at the Nyquist rate, sends **3.1× fewer bits than the proposed design**.

## 1. Problem, proposal and approach

**The application.** Floating electrochemical sensors (Fig. 2) report the glucose concentration and the cell density inside bioreactors over an open radio link to the process control loop. Yasar *et al.* [1] protect such a sensor inside its delta-sigma ADC (the S-DSM): every 1-bit modulator output is XORed with a bit of a 16-bit LFSR keystream. Security costs 24 µW (1.8 % of the chip power without security, the overhead measure of [1]), and because the bits are sent without decimation the wireless energy is reported to be up to 2.67× lower than that of a design that decimates and then encrypts with Trivium.

**Wider applications.** The chip's three modes, cyclic voltammetry, amperometry and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy, underlie electrochemical sensing well beyond bioreactors (Section I-A):

* **medical monitoring:** blood-glucose meters and continuous glucose monitors (amperometric enzyme electrodes); lactate, oxygen and pH in blood and sweat; and neurotransmitters such as dopamine, measured by fast-scan cyclic voltammetry;
* **implants and wearables:** impedance monitoring of implanted electrodes and the surrounding tissue, and sweat-sensing patches;
* **diagnostics, food and water:** point-of-care biosensors for bacteria, viruses and proteins, and voltammetric detection of heavy metals and other contaminants;
* **energy and industry:** state-of-health estimation of batteries and fuel cells, and monitoring of corrosion and electroplating baths.

In medical use the readings are personal health data and can drive therapy, such as an insulin dose, so confidentiality and integrity matter even more there. The secure link studied here applies in principle to any such sensor whose current is digitised by a delta-sigma modulator and transmitted wirelessly.

<p align="center"><b>Fig. 2.</b> The application: floating wireless sensors that report over an open radio link to the receiver of the control loop.</p>

**Research questions.** The evaluation in [1] covers an eavesdropper with a replica chip and two cryptanalyses; its key is stored, its keystream linear and its frames unauthenticated. The notebook asks four questions and answers them with open-source tools and the SkyWater SKY130 PDK:

1. **How secure is the S-DSM against a realistic attacker**, one who may also search the key space, learn a few keystream bits, obtain a chip or transmit on the link?
2. **Can the stored key be eliminated and every frame authenticated, without changing the analog front end, and at what cost?** The proposed design regenerates a 128-bit key at every power-up from SRAM cells (an SRAM physically unclonable function, PUF) through a BCH fuzzy extractor and a hash, replaces the LFSR by Grain-128a, and authenticates every frame with Grain-128a's MAC, a frame counter and a session nonce.
3. **Do transistor-level circuits change these answers?** The two SKY130 circuits that set the analog behaviour, the modulator's StrongARM comparator and the 6T SRAM-PUF cell, are simulated from extracted layouts across process, voltage and temperature (PVT) and carried to the accuracy, power and security of the sensor.
4. **Can the key generator be derived from a specification, and do the models hold together?** A compiler turns the reliability and secrecy specification into a generated decoder, verified in RTL simulation and laid out, and one mixed-level simulation of the complete sensor tests the abstractions behind the system-level results.

## 2. Results and discussion

The results follow the order of the notebook: the model and its consistency check, the block level, the system level, security, cost, the compiler and the mixed-level simulation.

### 2.1 Consistency check against the measurements of [1] (Sections V and VI)

**Method.** The model's two-channel experiment reproduces Fig. 4(c) of [1]: two modulators encrypt the same input with the same key, and the fraction of bits that differ is computed for every combination of dither, first-integrator noise and inter-channel mismatch. The energy model reproduces the table of Fig. 4(b) of [1].

**Result.** With dither, noise and 0.10 % FS mismatch, 50.0 % (zero input) and 48.2 % (same message) of the bits differ, against 50.05 % and 49.8 % measured; the ratios of transmitted bits behind the energy table match the energy ratios of [1] within 0.18 % (Fig. 4). On the same message, the model's offset alone gives 48.2 %, thermal noise without offset 26.2 %, and the model's simplified dither alone 4.8 %.

**Discussion.** For independent channels the same-message value is expected at 48.4 % with the model's test sine (an expected value, not a bound), so the agreement is a consistency check rather than a validation. [1] attributes the disagreement to its dither. The model represents the dither in a simplified form, in which it changes only 4.8 % of the bits on its own, so the model reaches this independence value (48.2 %; 49.8 % measured in [1]) only with a small offset between the channels (from 0.01 % of full scale upwards); this is a limit of the model, not a finding about the chip. Analog differences between chips are therefore the central question for the security of the original design, which Section VIII answers.

**Result.** The comparator takes six noiseless decisions down to ±0.2 mV correctly (Fig. 6). Its noise is the kT/C noise of the integrating nodes, set by the input pair: with only the input pair noisy, $\sigma$ = 0.516 mV, 94 % of the noise power, against 0.128 mV for the latch, tail and reset devices together, and the first-order formula at the simulated operating point gives 0.41–0.70 mV (Fig. 7). The extraction changes no quantity beyond its statistical error except the clock energy, a 0.1 mV systematic offset and, possibly, the SRAM cell's 85 °C error rate (3.14 % against 2.31 %, 2.2 standard errors paired). Across the 45 PVT conditions (Fig. 9) the comparator spans $\sigma_\mathrm{n}$ 0.37–0.81 mV, $\sigma_\mathrm{os}$ 4.01–5.85 mV and 144–822 fJ per decision; temperature dominates its noise and offset, and its energy is dynamic (E/$V_\mathrm{DD}^2$ 59–70 fF at 27 °C) except at 125 °C at the fast-nMOS corners, where leakage raises it to 210 fF. The SRAM cell's error rate rises on both sides of 25 °C (Fig. 7). Across corners the cell is stable at tt and ss, noisier at sf, and at ff and fs its errors at 85 °C are mostly reproducible flips (25.6 % of the cells at fs, 8.4 % at ff), which a closed-form error law explains as a rotation of the mismatch by up to 50° (Fig. 10). A recorded check with floating or grounded bit lines and without pass-gate mismatch leaves these flips unchanged within one cell in 128 and obtains the rotation directly from the simulation (53° at fs, against 58° from the error rate), and a supply-ramp sweep (0.1–100 µs) changes about 4 % of the cells against the 1 µs enrolment. Halving the time steps and tightening the relative tolerance tenfold changes none of 300 post-layout comparator decisions and none of 128 post-layout power-up bits (live convergence check).

**Discussion.** The SKY130 values lower the PUF noise-to-mismatch ratio by a factor of 2.7 compared with the commonly assumed 0.06, but the temperature dependence is stronger than assumed, and the symmetric linear law $s(T) = s_{25}\,(1 + a\,\lvert T - 25\rvert)$ underestimates the 85 °C rate (1.75 % against 3.14 %). The code is therefore sized from the simulated error rates over 1,000 Monte Carlo chips of a two-component cell model (noise and mismatch rotation) fitted to the post-layout SPICE Monte Carlo of the cell: a key failure below $10^{-6}$ from 0 to 85 °C needs t = 39 at the 99th percentile (33–46 over the 95 % interval of the 85 °C rate). Secrecy is bounded from below: with the 95 % lower bound on the min-entropy (0.908 bit per cell) and the 192 bits that a 128-bit key needs with a hash margin (ε = $2^{-32}$), at most t = 35 is admissible. **With 600 cells and a 25 °C enrolment neither requirement is met with margin**, so t = 45, which leaves 94–137 secret bits, is a working setting for the system simulations; the two-temperature enrolment (Section VIII-F) and the compiled codes (Section XI) size the key generator. Two further design rules follow from the block level: at the fast-nMOS corners temperature reverses the preference of cells rather than making them noisier, so a design verified at tt and 25 °C is not sufficient; and the key must be reconstructed with the supply ramp it was enrolled with.

**Ageing (Section VII-G; recorded, assumed NBTI law).** The PDK has no ageing model, so the ageing is emulated: the threshold shift of the pull-up that is on, from a published NBTI law scaled to the SKY130 oxide, supply and temperature, is imposed on each of the 1,024 extracted cells as a source in series with its gate, and the post-layout Monte Carlo is re-run with the same mismatch and noise seeds as the fresh run. With the cells holding their power-up values, the shift of a stable cell reaches 4.16 mV in ten years and the BER at 25 °C rises from 0.99 % to 1.53 % (85 °C: 3.14 % to 4.68 %); writing the inverse of each read back lowers it to 0.72 %. A per-cell calibration of each cell's mismatch referred to the pull-up gate predicts every aged error rate of the nominal law within 0.20 percentage points (1.08 with the pessimistic law), so the cell model carries the ageing to the key (Section 2.3).

### 2.3 System level: from transistor to system (Section VIII)

**Method.** The model of the complete sensor, with its SKY130 parameters, carries every circuit quantity to the system's own metrics: the SNDR of the decoded reading, the sensor's power, the success of the replica attack on the original design and the key-failure probability of the proposed design. The replica attacker owns a second chip, XORs the ciphertext with that chip's bits and runs Berlekamp-Massey; the notebook judges security against a **full-scan attacker** who tries every 64-bit window of up to four frames, with independent seeds for every chip pair and 95 % Clopper–Pearson intervals, and counts a setting as protected only when none of at least 100 pairs is broken (the 80-window attacker of [1] is reported alongside). Each of the six studies first predicts its effect with a first-order theory (describing function of the 1-bit quantiser, offset as an initial-state shift, clean-window bound, leaky-integrator NTF, scale invariance in $g_2$, the SRAM-PUF error law) and then simulates it at the 45 PVT conditions and far beyond them.

**The complete sensor, end to end.** One frame of the proposed design passes through every block with the SKY130 values, at the nominal condition and with every block at the sf corner and 85 °C, the condition of the noisiest comparator. The tag verifies, every bit is delivered, and the SNDR is 60.0 and 60.1 dB. The key is recovered at both (13 and 40 cell errors against t = 45), but at sf and 85 °C a power-up yields a wrong key with probability 0.13, which is why study F sizes the key generator per corner. The bit-stream spectrum (Fig. 11) has a white floor set by the integrator's thermal noise (−126.8 dBFS per bin from 2 to 8 kHz) and, above it, the 40 dB-per-decade shaping of the second-order loop; the in-band noise is −66.5 dBFS at both conditions, so the shaped quantisation noise, not the circuit noise, sets the SNDR. Assumed impairments that the reference model lacks (10 ps rms DAC jitter, 50 kHz 1/f corner, HD3 = −70 dBc) raise the low-frequency floor by 19 dB and lower the SNDR by 0.5 dB, so the 60 dB of the model is an optimistic estimate for a chip.

**Result.**
* *Accuracy* (study A, Fig. 12). The SNDR is 60.3–60.4 dB at all 45 PVT conditions. The comparator noise would have to grow 88-fold (to about 72 mV) before 3 dB are lost; an offset of 3× the largest PVT $\sigma_\mathrm{os}$ moves the decoded reading by 0.47 ppm of full scale. The describing-function theory places the −3 dB point within a factor of 1.4 with no fitted parameter.
* *Security of the original design* (studies B, D and E; Figs. 13 and 14). The full-scan attacker breaks 360 of 360 chip pairs at the PVT conditions: two chips disagree on 25 % of their bits, yet 4.9 % of the 64-bit windows remain clean, because the disagreements come in bursts. Comparator noise protects the design only from $\sigma_\mathrm{n}$ = 160 mV, 197× its largest PVT value, at a cost of 7.5 dB of SNDR; the 80-window attacker of [1] already fails on most pairs from about 60 mV, so judging protection by that attacker would understate the required noise by more than 2×. No offset protects it (24 of 100 pairs still broken at 3 V, beyond the supply); integrator noise leaves 97 of 100 pairs broken at 1,000× its value; capacitor-ratio mismatch still 16 of 100 at σ = 50 %.
* *Power* (study C). The simulated SKY130 comparator draws 1.4–8.2 µW, at most 1.6 % of the 510 µW measured modulator of [1], and is common to every design compared.
* *Modulator requirements* (study D). The SNDR is quantisation-limited (integrator-noise margin 68×); the OTA needs a DC gain of 40 dB or more (theory: 36 dB).
* *Security of the proposed design* (study F, Fig. 15). With a 25 °C enrolment and t = 45, all simulated chips meet $10^{-6}$ over 0–85 °C at tt and ss, 72.9 % at sf and none at ff and fs; the 99th-percentile chip needs t = 33, 29, 55, 89 and more than 120 at tt, ss, sf, ff and fs, against a secrecy limit of t ≤ 27 at the worst corner's min-entropy lower bound (t ≤ 35 with the tt bound of Section 2.2). An enrolment at 25 and 85 °C leaves the cells that flip at −40 °C (held-out error rate up to 3.09 % at sf); an enrolment at −40 and 85 °C keeps 68.9–95.3 % of the cells (the model, not fitted to them, predicts within 1.3 percentage points) and holds the held-out error rate at or below 1.01 % at every corner and temperature. With it, t = 17 meets $10^{-6}$ for at least 99.7 % of the chips at every corner and leaves 284–352 secret bits at fs, with 935 cells for 600 kept cells.
* *Ageing of the key* (study F, part 3, Fig. 16; recorded, assumed NBTI law, tt). The PDK has no ageing model, so the ageing is emulated on the 1,024 extracted cells (Section VII-G) with a published NBTI law scaled to the SKY130 oxide, supply and temperature (2.83 mV after one year, n = 1/6; pessimistic 7.88 mV, n = 1/4). An array kept powered with its power-up values ages its cells towards the threshold, and the fresh margin of both codes is small (the 99th-percentile chip needs t = 39 of 45, or 12 of 17): the $10^{-6}$ target is lost after 26 days (t = 45) and 23 days (t = 17), or after 7 and 8 hours with the pessimistic law. Writing the inverse of each read back reverses the drift, and the key-failure probability falls with age; an array powered for 1 % of the time keeps t = 45 for 7.0 years. The write-back costs the chip nonce its randomness: one read of the median chip falls from 11.8 to 4.4 bits of collision entropy in ten years.

**Discussion.** In the original design, the analog quantities that could make two chips differ enough to stop the attack do so only at a direct cost in accuracy, and the others never do: its security must come from a key. In the proposed design, the only circuit that matters for security is the SRAM cell, and what it requires, a code and an enrolment whose two temperatures bracket the operating range, sized for the worst corner, is a system-level design decision rather than a circuit modification. Because the corners at risk cannot be identified at room temperature (fs has the lowest error rate of all corners at 25 °C), key reconstruction must also be tested hot. The comparator needs no design change; the integrators need an OTA gain of about 1.2× the oversampling ratio.

**Result.** The original design fails 11 of the 15 attacks (Fig. 18); six of them (A7, A8, A9, A13, A14, A15) need an attacker who holds the chip or transmits on the link, beyond the eavesdropper that [1] evaluates, and A6 assumes that the LFSR restarts from the key in every frame, which [1] does not state. Six breach the data: brute force (A2), Berlekamp-Massey recovery (A3), the replica attack with a full scan (A4; seed confirmed at a replica trim of +0.03 % FS), multi-frame saturation (A6; 21 frames, keystream estimate 98.4 % correct), key extraction (A7) and keystream reuse (A15; reading recovered with correlation 0.95 over 11 replayed sessions). A5 leaks the amplitude of the reading (0.246 against 0.249 FS). Four place fake data on the server: spoofing, the clone, replay and bit-flipping (Fig. 17). In the model, the proposed design stops 14 of the 15 at the working setting (7 verdicts simulated, 4 analytic, 4 by design); the fifteenth, helper-data leakage (A11), leaves 94 secret bits at the 95 % lower bound of the min-entropy against the 192 required, which the one-time enrolment at −40 and 85 °C raises to 284-352 bits, so that it resists ten; it detects and rejects five (four in simulation; the clone, A9, by design, through the unclonability of the PUF); without its chip nonce it would fall to A15 (correlation 0.95), with it the correlation is −0.03. The chip nonce is hashed from 64 power-up reads of the SRAM array, because one read holds only 9.3 bits of collision entropy at tt and 25 °C and 1.1 bits at fs and −40 °C (41 distinct chip nonces in 4,000 sessions of the worst chip, against 4,000 with 64 reads; Section VIII-F). The helper-data oracle accepted 0 of 200 tampered power-ups and answered every query exactly as a chip with a different fingerprint. The parameter sweeps show how thin the analog margins of the original design are against the attacker of [1] alone: the replica attack succeeds at 10 of 56 grid points, one seed each (including 0.03 % FS replica offset with the dither of [1], one grid step below the assumed offset) and saturation at 21 of 81 (every point with buffer gain ≥ 1.0 and forced current ≥ 2 µA); the proposed design is breached at none of the 137 points. A fixed-versus-random leakage test on a register-level power model (Section IX-D, Welch's $t$ against the threshold 4.5) finds that neither design resists power analysis as built: the LFSR of [1] leaks at 387 of its 512 clocks ($\lvert t\rvert$ up to 43.7), Grain-128a only during its initialisation (up to 14.1, and 2.6 after it), with both controls below the threshold.

**Discussion.** The failures of the original design have three architectural causes: its key is short, linear and stored; its keystream restarts in every frame, so any repetition of message, forced input or session exposes it; and its frames carry neither authentication nor freshness. The analog attacks evaluated in [1] (A4, A5) and the saturation attack (A6) succeed once the attacker uses the capabilities of the threat model, as Section VIII found for the comparator and the integrators. The proposed design's security rests entirely on digital blocks (the PUF key generator, Grain-128a, the MAC and the counter), whose cost Section X determines. The published fast correlation attacks on Grain-128a need $2^{113.8}$ [18] and $2^{106.3}$ [19] keystream bits, far beyond one frame, and [18] excludes the authenticated mode used here.

**Power analysis (Sections IX-D and X-H; outside the threat model).** A first-order fixed-versus-random leakage test (Welch's $t$, threshold 4.5, 2,000 traces per group, with a control of two random-key groups) is run on a register-level power model of both keystream generators and on their placed gate-level netlists. Neither design resists power analysis as built, but they expose their keys in different places. The LFSR of [1] leaks at 387 of its 512 clocks in the register-level model ($\lvert t\rvert$ up to 43.7) and, at gate level, at the updates of its key LFSR ($\lvert t\rvert$ up to 20.5), in every frame, because it restarts from the key without a nonce. The proposed design leaks only at the start: in the register-level model during the first 125 clocks of Grain-128a's initialisation ($\lvert t\rvert$ up to 14.1), and at gate level during the serial key load (up to 22.3) and the first initialisation updates (up to 31.7); while it encrypts and authenticates the data it stays below the threshold (at most 2.6 and 2.4). Both controls stay below it. The test locates the exposure, it does not give a security level (no glitches or internal power are modelled): the proposed design's countermeasure, a masked or parallel key load and masked first initialisation rounds, would act only in these first clocks.

### 2.5 Implementation cost (Section X)

**Method.** The Verilog of every design is verified against published test vectors at RTL and gate level (178 of 178 checks pass), in two forms: as written for one bit per clock, and in a low-power form given equally to every design (a radix-16 parallel state update, which updates the cipher state once every 16 clocks, and clock gating of every register in the cycles in which it is not written). Each is synthesised with Yosys on `sky130_fd_sc_hd`, placed with its clock tree with OpenROAD and simulated at gate level on its own test job (tt, 25 °C, 1.8 V, 10 MHz); its power comes from the switching activity of that simulation. The low-power block of the proposed design is also routed in a recorded run (`ROUTE_BLOCK`), checked with Magic DRC, the antenna rules and netgen LVS, extracted with OpenRCX, timed at the slow, typical and fast corners, and re-simulated from the routed netlist. **No number is scaled from one process to another, and no modelled number enters the figure of merit.** The process enters only through a proposed figure of merit, FOM = $E_\mathrm{bit}/(E_\mathrm{NAND2}\,S_\Sigma)$: the gate-level energy per bit of the security logic (the dither LFSRs of [1] removed) over $E_\mathrm{NAND2}$ = 7.93 fJ, the energy of one switching of the library's minimum NAND2 at fan-out 1 (from the SKY130 Liberty tables; $E_\mathrm{bit}$ and $E_\mathrm{NAND2}$ both scale with C $V^2$, which cancels), per bit of security delivered, summed over the goals met (cipher strength by the best attack applicable as used: S-DSM 10, Trivium 80, Grain-128a 128; MAC tag 32; chip nonce 31; key protection on a captured chip, 128 for a key present only while powered, 0 for a stored key); dimensionless, lower is better, computed only for the blocks of this flow. No radio is designed; with the radio of [1] as an example, the average power of a sensor sending one frame per minute is given as well, and the transmission is compared by the bits each architecture sends per reading at OSR 50.

Placed unscaled on the chip of [1] (Fig. 21), its SKY130 power raises the chip from 1,376 to 1,416 µW: overhead (added power relative to the chip without its security block) 4.8 % against 1.8 % for the S-DSM and 16.3 % for Trivium, or 4.5 %, 1.7 % and 14.0 % of the chip's total. A leakage test on the placed gate-level netlists (Section X-H; switching energy of every net with its placement capacitance, 2,000 traces per group) finds the proposed design's key exposed during its serial key load and its first initialisation updates ($\lvert t\rvert$ up to 22.3 and 31.7) and not in the keystream and MAC phase (at most 2.4), and the S-DSM's at its LFSR updates (up to 20.5).

**Discussion.** The FOM and the power ratio answer different questions: per NAND2 switching and per security bit delivered, the proposed design has the lowest FOM of the blocks of this flow (2.21, 2.3× below Trivium and 6.4× below the S-DSM; it leads Trivium unless the tag, nonce and key-protection bits count for under 5.6 % of their value); in absolute power its whole block costs 3.6× the S-DSM in the same flow. The factor of 3.6 is the price of 256 bits of cipher state and 64 bits of MAC state against 16 bits of LFSR state per register, and of the parts that must run at the 10 MHz modulator clock; to first order the process does not enter it. In transmitted bits the proposed design keeps the position of the S-DSM: **1.44–1.60× fewer than the conventional design of [1]**, which sends 16-bit words at 4.5–5× the Nyquist rate. That advantage does not hold against an information-equivalent design: decimating on the sensor and encrypting 16-bit words at the Nyquist rate with Grain-128a + MAC sends **16.04 bits per reading, 3.1× fewer than the proposed design**. What the bit-level architecture keeps is the absence of a decimator and a word-level datapath on the sensor, not the fewest bits per reading. With the radio of [1] as an example, the radio dominates, yet at one frame per minute the proposed design averages 28.0 µW (9.2 µW for on-sensor decimation).

A mutation test plants 19 single faults in the generated decoder of specification C: the 12 directed tests catch all of them, each fault by 2 to 12 tests, and two controls that change only code that never executes survive. The decoder's GF(2<sup>10</sup>) multiplier, through which every syndrome, locator and Chien step passes, is proven for every input: for each of the specifications D, C and A, all 1,048,576 operand pairs give the model's product, and a SAT proof (Yosys, MiniSAT; recorded) finds no pair on which it differs from an independent reference.

**Discussion.** The search turns the qualitative finding of Section VIII into the result of a systematic grid search under the cell models (r = 1, 3, 5, 7; n in steps of 32 up to 1023; every t up to ⌊n/10⌋ − 1): **the two-temperature enrolment is what makes an all-corner specification achievable**, and its cleaner fingerprint needs so weak a code that the all-corner design is no larger than the typical-corner one. Without repetition the BCH-only designs would need t = 21–36 and, by the compiler's estimate, 1.5–2.2× the area. The size of the key generator, with a decoder twice (D) or about equal to (C) the low-power keystream block and a hash of 15,097 GE, is a property of this work's round-per-clock SHA-256 and serial flip-flop decoder template, not of PUF key generation: a lightweight hash and a microcoded decoder, as in PUFKY [14], would be several times smaller. With t = 4, the success flag misses a read beyond the code's capacity with a probability of about 4 × 10⁻⁴ (theory; none of 2,000 simulated reads); a wrong key that escapes it would still be detected by the receiver when the first frame's MAC fails.

### 2.7 Full-chain mixed-level simulation of the complete sensor (Section XII)

**Method.** One simulation runs every block at the level at which its behaviour matters: the SRAM array as 640 extracted 6T cells in ngspice, the key decoder generated by the compiler and the Grain-128a/MAC block as RTL in Icarus Verilog, two chips' modulators with their post-layout StrongARM comparators inside the loop in ngspice, and the receiver and attackers in Python. Every decision of the transistor-level comparator is tested against the behavioural threshold model on the input it actually saw, with the number of disagreements that noise explains computed from theory. The live run covers 1,000 clocks at tt and 37 °C (a settings form chooses others); step 6, the replica attack over 30,000 clocks per chip, is a recorded run (`LONG_RUN`, 72 min on two cores).

**Result.** In the field read at 37 °C, 7 of the 600 transistor-level SRAM cells differ from the enrolment (1.17 %; 7.9 ± 2.8 expected from the recorded Monte Carlo); the generated RTL decoder corrects them, bit-identical to the model, and the server's key is recovered after 91,127 clock cycles (9.1 ms at 10 MHz). In the published run 3 of the 2,000 decisions of the two post-layout comparators differ from the threshold model, consistent with their noise (2.4 expected, 3 or more with probability 43 %; 0 to 3 in development runs not shown here), and the transistor-level loop decodes to the same reading as the model (Fig. 23(b), (c), (f)). The RTL keystream block encrypts and authenticates exactly as the model, down to the 32-bit tag; the server accepts the frame, a frame with one changed bit is rejected, and an eavesdropper with a wrong key fails the MAC check and agrees with the true bits in 47.6 % of the clocks. The 1,000-clock replica attack is illustrative only (its 80 attempts fall on 23 distinct, overlapping windows). Over 30,000 clocks (recorded, Fig. 23(i)–(k)) the two chips disagree on 24.8 % of the bits and 5.1 % of the 64-bit windows are clean (behavioural model on the same pair: 3.9–4.6 % over 20 noise seeds); Berlekamp-Massey recovers the original design's key from 8 of 80 windows and the proposed design's from none. On the frame itself (Fig. 23(l)–(n)), a search of all 65,535 keys of the original design with the replica's transistor-level bits as the plaintext guess ranks the true key first (95.2 % agreement against 50.0 ± 1.6 % for the wrong keys); against the proposed design neither 1,000 random keys nor the 128 keys one bit from the true key do better than chance (50.1 ± 1.6 % and 49.8 ± 1.7 %); the receiver rejects all 1,080 single-bit forgeries of the RTL frame and 100,000 random tags, while the original design accepts every payload change; and the ciphertext passes four NIST SP 800-22 tests (p = 0.06 to 0.93) that the modulator bits fail (p ≤ 3 × 10⁻²⁶).

**Discussion.** The abstraction behind the system-level results holds where it could fail: in the closed loop, the post-layout transistor-level comparator shows no memory, kickback or metastability effect at this length (2,000 decisions cannot reveal an effect rarer than about one in a thousand), and the key generator works on transistor-level cells with its decoder simulated in RTL. The transistor-level pair is slightly burstier than the behavioural model, so, for this pair at this condition, the behavioural statistics of Section VIII slightly underestimate the windows available to the attacker, not the reverse. The replica result confirms, for one chip pair at one condition, that the analog differences between two chips do not protect the original design. The recorded long run predates the correction of the first-integrator noise (it uses twice the corrected value, which if anything makes the attack harder) and uses the 600-cell, t = 45 decoder rather than the compiled two-temperature design.

## 3. Conclusions, limitations and future work

**Conclusions** (the four questions of Section I).
1. **In the model, the S-DSM is not secure against a realistic attacker.** With a simplified representation of the dither of [1] and an assumed 0.1 % FS input offset between the chips, it withstands the 80-window attack of [1] (which, without that offset, breaks 356 of 360 simulated chip pairs at the 45 PVT conditions), but not once the attacker scans every window, and it leaks the reading's amplitude to the same-message analysis, protected by the decorrelation between the victim's and the replica's bits; an attacker who scans the whole capture, trims the replica or votes over frames removes that protection, and in all it fails 11 of the 15 attacks.
2. **The stored key can be eliminated and every frame authenticated** without changing the front end: the PUF-keyed, authenticated design stops 14 of the 15 attacks in the model at the working setting (and, with the one-time −40/85 °C enrolment, resists 10 and detects 5 of all 15) (7 verdicts simulated, 4 analytic, 4 by design); the fifteenth, helper-data leakage (A11), leaves 94 secret bits at the 95 % lower bound of the min-entropy against the 192 required, which the two-temperature enrolment meets (284-352 bits). The cost is digital power (3.6× the S-DSM under strictly equal treatment; 4.5 % of [1]'s chip power with the unscaled SKY130 block; proposed FOM 2.21, the lowest of the blocks re-synthesised in the same flow, against 5.09 for Trivium and 14.1 for the S-DSM) and area (9,869 GE for the low-power keystream block, 47,292 GE for the designed security subsystem with the −40/85 °C key generator). The transmitted bits stay at the S-DSM's level, 1.44–1.60× fewer than [1]'s conventional design at OSR 50, but 3.1× more than an information-equivalent design that decimates before encrypting; no radio energy is claimed.
3. **The simulated SKY130 circuits do not change the accuracy, and do not secure the original design**: 60.3–60.4 dB SNDR with the comparator noise of each of the 45 PVT conditions (behavioural model; no offset, one noise seed each), and no analog quantity stops the full-scan replica attacker without a direct loss of accuracy. **They do change the key**: with 600 cells enrolled at 25 °C neither the reliability target nor the 192-bit secret budget is met with margin, and at the fast-nMOS corners the mismatch rotates with temperature; the key generator must be enrolled at both ends of the temperature range (−40 and 85 °C: t = 17, 284–352 secret bits at fs, the corner with the lowest min-entropy bound) and sized for its worst corner.
4. **A compiler derives a key generator that meets the target at all five corners**, verified bit for bit and routed, and **the abstractions hold** for one chip pair at one condition in a mixed-level simulation of the complete sensor with transistor-level SRAM cells and comparators, which also reproduces the replica attack on the original design over 30,000 clocks (recorded).

**Implications beyond this design** (notebook Section XV-E).
1. **Analog differences between chips are not a key:** a full-scan replica attacker breaks 360 of 360 PVT pairs of the S-DSM; only 160 mV of comparator noise (197× the PVT maximum, 7.5 dB of SNDR) would stop it.
2. **A PUF key and a PUF nonce need opposite cells:** one power-up read holds 1.1 bits of nonce entropy at fs and −40 °C, so the chip nonce needs 64 reads for 68 bits. Ageing pulls them further apart: the inverted write-back that keeps the key reliable lowers one read's entropy (median chip, tt) from 11.8 to 4.4 bits in ten years.
3. **Enrol a PUF at both ends of its temperature range:** a 25 °C enrolment meets reliability and secrecy at no corner; −40 and 85 °C does, with t = 17 and 284–352 secret bits.
4. **Optimise the baselines as far as the proposed design:** with the same low-power techniques the proposed design's power ratio to the S-DSM falls from 9.7× to 3.6×.
5. **Pin every SPICE process to one core:** unpinned ngspice processes ran up to 3.5× slower, without a warning.
6. **Make every failed measurement stop the run:** every extraction raises an error instead of returning a clamped or empty value.

**Limitations.**
* **Simulation, not fabrication.** The circuit results are SKY130 simulations of extracted layouts; the comparison with silicon relies on the measurements reported in [1].
* **The SRAM noise is thermal only** (no 1/f, random-telegraph or supply noise; ageing only with an assumed BTI law, Section VII-G), so $s_{25}$ = 0.0225 likely underestimates the noise of silicon (the PDK mismatch is not silicon-verified either); with the commonly assumed 0.06 a 600-cell design would need t = 60, which leaves no secret bits. Spatial correlation between cells is not modelled.
* **The SRAM-PUF corner analysis uses 512 cells and two held-out power-ups per corner**, which resolve error rates of about 0.1 %, and covers process × temperature (−40, 25 and 85 °C) at the nominal supply only; the supply-ramp effect is simulated on 128 cells at one corner. The compiler's specification C uses the enrolment at 25 and 85 °C; Section VIII-F shows that −40 and 85 °C is needed to also remove the cells that flip in the cold, and that key generator (specification D) is compiled as sized, not searched by the compiler.
* **The layouts are single cells drawn with the logic design rules**; the comparator's output buffers and SR latch remain schematic; the analog extraction is capacitance only. The modulator's integrators, TIA and buffer are behavioural (with thermal noise, saturation, OTA gain and capacitor mismatch as parameters).
* **Digital power is computed at the typical corner** with zero-delay activity (no glitches), and is not scaled to 65 nm. The side-channel tests (register-level model, Section IX-D; placed netlists, Section X-H) locate where the key is exposed; neither design has a countermeasure. Timing covers three library corners without on-chip variation or IR drop. The key-reconstruction unit's detailed routing is a recorded run; the array periphery, the downlink receiver, the hash's padding and control and the power of the decoder and hash are not costed, and the blocks are not integrated into one layout.
* **The mixed-level simulation covers one chip pair at one condition** per run; its 30,000-clock replica run is recorded, with twice the corrected first-integrator noise and the t = 45 decoder.
* **Assumptions about [1] and the working setting.** The model simplifies the dither of [1], and the original design's protection against the attacks of [1] rests on an assumed 0.1 % FS offset between the chips' inputs; [1] does not state how its key is held, so A7 assumes non-volatile memory and A6 and A15 an LFSR that restarts from the key at every frame. The system simulations use t = 45 with a 25 °C enrolment, which fails A11; the security claims rest on the key generator enrolled at −40 and 85 °C (t = 17).
* **No radio and no proof of security.** No radio is designed or simulated: the designs are compared in transmitted bits per reading, with the radio of [1] once as a labelled example. Of the proposed design's 15 verdicts, 7 are simulated, 4 analytic and 4 hold by design; none is a formal proof. Invasive and fault attacks, PUF cloning and remanence read-out are outside the threat model.
* **Only the two designs of [1] are re-synthesised for the like-for-like comparison;** the crypto engines, physical-layer designs and PUF key generators are quoted as published, in their own 14–65 nm nodes, which favours them.

**Future work.** A transistor-level SKY130 integrator and TIA in the mixed-level simulation; an SRAM-PUF cell model with 1/f and random-telegraph noise and ageing measured on silicon, with the compiler re-run against it and dark-bit masking [15] as an alternative to the two-temperature enrolment; the compiled two-temperature key generator, with its array, in the mixed-level simulation at the fast-nMOS corners; the complete security subsystem (key-reconstruction unit, a lightweight hash, the MAC delay line and the keystream block) in one layout with the power of every block; and a tape-out in a node comparable to the prior works, where the power can be measured rather than simulated.
